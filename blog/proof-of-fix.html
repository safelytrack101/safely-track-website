What Counts as Proof of Fix (and What Doesn't)

A corrective action is closed when someone can show, months later and to a stranger, that the hazard was fixed, by whom, and when. "Fixed, per the super" in a spreadsheet cell does not pass that test. A photo of the same spot after the fix, a name, and a date usually does.

Most safety programs are good at finding hazards. The weak point is the other end: the record that says the hazard went away. That record is what an auditor, an insurer, a client, or an attorney asks for, and it is the one most often missing. We call this the closeout gap.

The four things a closed item needs

Strip away the software and the forms, and a defensible closeout has four parts:

  1. An after photo of the same spot. Framed like the original, so a reader can put the two side by side. A photo of "a guardrail" proves nothing if it could be any guardrail on the site.
  2. Who fixed it. A named person, not "the electrical sub." If a crew did the work, the foreman who signed off.
  3. When it was fixed. A timestamp captured when the evidence was taken, not a date typed in later.
  4. What was done. One sentence. "Installed midrail and toeboard on east edge, level 3." This matters when the fix was a workaround rather than the obvious repair.

Who should supply the evidence

The person closest to the fix. That is almost never the safety professional who found the hazard. If the consultant or safety manager has to go back out and photograph every fix themselves, two things happen: closeouts lag by days or weeks, and the safety person quietly stops doing it for low-severity items.

The catch is that the people closest to the fix are foremen, subcontractors, and maintenance techs. They do not have a login to your safety software, and they are not going to make one. Any closeout process that starts with "first, have the sub sign in" fails in practice.

Collecting proof from people with no login

What works is meeting them where they already are, which is a phone with a camera and a text inbox:

  • Send the item to them directly. One message per item, with the original photo, the location, and what is being asked. Not a weekly PDF with forty items in it.
  • Make the reply the evidence. A link that opens a camera, takes the after photo, and asks for one line of what was done. No account, no app install.
  • Stamp it on the way in. The name, time, and the item it belongs to should be attached automatically, so nobody has to file it.
  • Let the safety person accept or reject. A submitted fix is not a closed item until someone qualified looks at it. A rejected fix should go back to the same person with a note.
A useful rule

If you cannot produce the after photo for a closed item in under a minute, treat it as open. That one rule surfaces most of the closeout gap in an existing program.

Items that cannot be photographed

Some fixes are procedural: a retrained operator, a revised permit, an updated SDS binder. Proof here is a document, not a photo: the sign-in sheet, the revised page, the training record. The same four parts apply. Who, when, what was done, and an artifact someone can open later.

What to do with overdue items

An open item with a due date in the past is a bigger liability than one that was never written up, because it shows the hazard was known. Two habits help:

  • Escalate on a schedule, not on memory. A reminder at the due date, then to the person's supervisor a few days later.
  • Report overdue items to the client or site lead as their own section, not buried in the full list. People fix what is visible.
