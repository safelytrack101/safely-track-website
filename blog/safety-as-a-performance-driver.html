Safety as a Performance Driver: What HBR's Research Means for Safety Consultants

The short version

Executives call safety a core value and then budget for it as a cost. Research published in Harvard Business Review argues that framing costs companies money, and that safety pays off in customer satisfaction and sales. The fix the authors propose comes down to measuring the right things and rewarding the behavior that prevents harm. That is work a safety consultant can make visible to every client.

In the September-October 2024 issue of Harvard Business Review, Vikas Mittal and Alessandro Piazza of Rice University and Sonam Singh of the University of South Florida published Safety Should Be a Performance Driver. The subtitle says it plainly: it's more than just a compliance issue. It is worth reading in full. Here is our summary, and what we think it means for consulting firms and the clients they serve.

Valued, but not managed

The authors interviewed 76 executives at about 30 companies, in industries from health care and facilities management to oil-field services and manufacturing. The gap between what those executives said and what they did is the heart of the article:

  • Every one of them described safety as a cost.
  • 94% called it a core value.
  • 17% said it was part of their strategy.
  • 35% tracked any safety metric.
  • 4% tied safety metrics to incentives.

None of them could point to any evidence linking safety to customer satisfaction, sales, margins or retention. Anyone who has sold safety services will recognize this. The budget conversation starts from "what does compliance require," not "what is this worth to us."

Framing changes the budget

In a study by one of the authors, managers put 60% fewer resources into an investment when it was presented as a way to avoid future losses rather than a way to improve performance. The executives showed the same habit: most of them talked about AI as a growth investment and about safety as loss avoidance. "Avoid the fine" and "avoid the injury" sound important, but they lose to almost anything framed as a gain.

The case that safety pays

The article pulls together evidence that safety shows up in business results, not only in incident logs:

  • A 2021 study of 11,451 customers across 1,785 companies linked safety to a 9% lift in customer satisfaction, which in turn drove a 13% increase in sales.
  • A 19-year panel of 619 public firms found that better employee safety records went with higher customer satisfaction.
  • Across 32,061 New York City construction sites, mandatory safety training cut injuries by 15 to 18%, with a 42% return on investment.

The last one matters for anyone who delivers training. A 42% return is a number a CFO can work with. "Required by OSHA" is not.

Five steps

The authors lay out five steps for treating safety as a driver of performance. In our words:

  1. Align on meaning. Agree on what safety means in your business, for workers and for customers.
  2. Agree on metrics. Pick a small set of measures that connect to outcomes the business cares about.
  3. Anticipate and prevent problems. Find and fix hazards before they become incidents.
  4. Target training. Train specific preventive behaviors, not general awareness.
  5. Incentivize employees. Reward the behaviors that prevent harm, not only a good injury rate.

The examples are memorable. They include Alcoa under Paul O'Neill, and a Texas school district that tracked more than 60 safety metrics and found only five of them predicted bullying and physical safety. A Pennsylvania nursing home chain rebuilt its training around spotting bedsores early, and bedsore rates fell from 23.1% to 9.3%. A commercial construction firm entered workers who consistently wore their PPE into a monthly gift card drawing, tracked on a phone app, and compliance rose from 43% to more than 92% in six months.

The closing example is the one we'd show a client. A facilities management company found that safety drove 10% of what its customers valued. It defined safety as three specific behaviors, cut 40-plus mandatory trainings to five, tied site manager bonuses to those behaviors instead of accident counts, and made safety part of its sales pitch. Sales rose 24% over a year and a half.

The part we'd underline

Steps two through five are all about leading indicators: hazards found, corrective actions closed, preventive behaviors observed. Lagging injury rates can't tell you whether any of it is working until someone gets hurt.

What it means for safety consultants

Consulting firms are in an unusual position here. You are already on site, already finding hazards, already training crews. What usually goes missing is the record that turns that work into a number a client's leadership can see. A twelve-page visit report filed in a shared drive doesn't change how a client budgets for safety. A trend line does: observations per visit, how fast high-severity items get closed, which sites keep repeating the same findings.

That is how a consultant moves out of the compliance budget and into the performance conversation. If your client can see that corrective actions close in four days instead of three weeks, and that repeat findings at their worst site fell by half, you are no longer a cost they tolerate. You are the person showing them a metric only about a third of the executives in the HBR study said their companies tracked at all.

  • Pick a few leading indicators with each client and report them every month, the same way, so the trend is the story.
  • Count closeouts, not just findings. A hazard found and never fixed is the cost side of the ledger. A hazard closed with proof of fix is the performance side.
  • Note good practice too. Positive observations are the preventive behaviors step five says to reward. If you don't record them, nobody can reward them.
  • Tie training to what you saw. If the same finding keeps showing up, that is the training topic, and the next quarter's numbers show whether it worked.
